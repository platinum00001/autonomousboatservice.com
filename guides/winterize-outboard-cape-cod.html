How to winterize an outboard on Cape Cod

A practical outboard winterizing guide for Cape Cod boaters: fuel, flushing, gear oil, fogging, batteries and storage, plus when to call a shop.

Cape Cod winters aren't the coldest in New England, but they swing above and below freezing over and over. That freeze-and-thaw cycle is what cracks water passages and lets moisture creep into fuel and gearcases. A good layup takes a couple of hours and saves you a lot of grief in May.

Before you start: your engine's owner's manual is the final word. Makers differ on fogging, fuel and stabilizer for modern fuel-injected engines. If your engine is under warranty, have a dealer do it, or keep receipts for everything you use.

When to do it

Most Cape boats come out between late September and November. Winterize before the first hard freeze. If you're still boating in late October, at least flush and drain the engine after each trip.

What you need

  • Marine fuel stabilizer, and fresh fuel to top off
  • Flush muffs or a flush port adapter, and a garden hose
  • Lower-unit gear oil of the type your manual specifies, a pump and new drain-screw gaskets
  • Fogging oil, if your manual calls for it
  • Engine oil and filter (four-strokes), and a fuel-water separator filter
  • A battery charger or maintainer
  • Rags, a drain pan and grease for the grease points

Step by step

  1. Stabilize the fuel. Add stabilizer to the tank at the dose on the bottle, then top the tank off to about 90% to reduce condensation. Run the engine long enough to pull treated fuel through the whole system. Some owners drain portable tanks instead. Follow your manual.
  2. Flush with fresh water. Salt left in the cooling passages corrodes all winter. Flush on muffs or through the flush port until the water runs clean and warm. Never run the engine on muffs without water flowing, and keep clear of the prop.
  3. Change the engine oil and filter (four-strokes). Used oil carries acids that sit on bearings all winter. Change it now rather than in spring.
  4. Fog the engine, if your manual says to. Carbureted and older two-stroke engines usually get fogging oil. Many modern fuel-injected engines have their own procedure, or say not to fog through the intake. Check first.
  5. Change the lower-unit gear oil. Drain from the bottom screw with the top vent open. Milky or creamy oil means water got in through a seal. Get it fixed before it freezes and cracks the gearcase. Refill from the bottom until oil comes out the top vent.
  6. Replace the fuel-water separator. Cheap insurance against ethanol-related water problems.
  7. Grease and protect. Grease the swivel and tilt tubes and the steering points. Pull the prop, grease the shaft splines and check for fishing line behind the prop. Spray a corrosion inhibitor on the powerhead.
  8. Drain it and store it down. Lower the engine to vertical so all the water drains out of the cooling passages.
  9. Batteries. Charge them fully, disconnect them, and either take them home or use a maintainer. A flat battery can freeze.
  10. Cover it. Shrink-wrap or a good cover, with ventilation, keeps snow and moisture out.

When to call a shop

Call a shop if the gear oil is milky, the tell-tale was weak all season, you found fuel in the oil, or the engine is under warranty. A shop can also handle inboard and sterndrive blocks and manifolds, and the boat's fresh-water and head systems, which take more steps to protect.

Common questions

Do I need to fog a fuel-injected outboard?

It depends on the maker. Many fuel-injected four-strokes have their own storage procedure, and some advise against fogging through the intake. Check your owner's manual or ask a dealer.

Should I fill or drain the fuel tank for winter?

For built-in tanks, most guidance is to add stabilizer and fill to about 90% to limit condensation. Portable tanks are often drained or used up. Follow your engine maker's advice.
